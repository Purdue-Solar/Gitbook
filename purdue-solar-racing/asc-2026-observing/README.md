# ASC 2026 Observing

### Solar Car Convoy

The solar car convoy at its minimum consists of a chase vehicle, solar car, and lead vehicle. The chase vehicle is the brain of the convoy, you will have the chase driver probably instructing when to pull over for traffic dumps or repairs. The solar car driver can only have one radio comm, so they’re probably only communicating with the chase while the chase communicates with both solar and lead (have two different people on those radios in chase - faster than making the driver do it all). The chase vehicle acts as an extension behind the solar car; when solar turns, chase turns with it without stopping; when solar moves on from a stop sign, chase doesn’t need to stop on its own. Chase driver needs to be able to stay calm, make quick decisions, multitask, press all the right radio buttons, avoid hitting solar, and not get overwhelmed.

Lead vehicle has to have someone with a route book and route bulletins (last minute route changes) in front of them. Lead has to stay a solid distance (\~100m?) in front of the solar car to prevent drafting, and try to avoid leaving cars in between lead and solar.

Clear communication across the convoy is very important. Traffic has to be dumped (pull over the convoy and let cars pass) when 6 or more cars are trailing behind chase, which happens often when taking a solar car at 30 mph down a two-lane 55 mph road. Chase driver will radio about “blocking” which is when the chase moves to block traffic and protect solar while changing lanes or turning. Chase and lead will probably both have CB radios, which are pretty finicky unless you stick a giant antenna on your car, and are used to communicate with other solar teams’ convoys that need to pass or be passed. Test all your radios at far distances before using them. Washington had pretty bad comms the first few days, letting cars between chase and solar and whatnot, but they learned as they went along.

There’s a lot of safety equipment that must be in the chase/lead vehicles, and the chase also has to have the observer’s luggage in it. Account for that and don’t overcrowd the cars with passengers or stuff, because you will need access to equipment and luggage on the daily.

Being a solar car driver kinda sucks. It’s hot and you drive for hours upon hours on a hard seat, with a warm water pack to drink from and a big helmet to keep you even warmer.

### Food and Sleep

Food and sleep and showers are important for health, productivity, and morale. Your drivers cannot be falling asleep at the wheel. Camping may save money, but getting a giant Airbnb and letting everyone sleep on the air conditioned floor may be a worthwhile alternative. ASC is hot and sweaty and stinky, and the showers + AC + free breakfast of a hotel might do wonders. Have someone whose sole job it is to procure sleeping arrangements, be that booking and assigning people to hotel rooms or setting up and taking down tents - this person doesn’t do any work on the car, and it might be a good idea to have this be someone from outside the team (like a family member) so they don’t actually know how to fix the car. You may have to stop and sleep in between checkpoints, in which case have someone scout ahead and look for public spaces like school gyms or libraries or parking lots to camp at; oftentimes you can simply ask and they’ll let you use their air conditioned space for the night.&#x20;

The same “sole job” thing goes for food. Cooking can easily take up 6 hours a day (3 meals, two hours of prepping + cooking + cleaning up) and whoever does that should not be touching the car. If you want to take full advantage of driving time during the day, there will be no lunch break, so pack sandwiches or something in both chase and lead.

### Equipment

We found dead rats in some of the cooking stuff that hadn’t been touched since last summer yuck. It’s nice to be able to pack less, but with an unfinished car there’s only so much you can leave behind. The workspace outdoors gets very messy very quickly, especially when multiple subteams are working on stuff. Best practice is probably to establish to everyone where everything belongs (spreadsheet?), and put things away as soon as they’re done being used. Hardware organization is rough too. I liked carrying a sharpie around everywhere to label things. The mini-lathe and mini-mill are pretty useful (composites inserts, safety wire holes) and multiple teams asked if they could use them. Heat and bugs are terrible for morale, bring thermocells and bug spray and those bug tennis rackets and big tents for shade.

### Team

App State has every team member wake up around 6:45, all together no matter who is needed for charging. They have a lot of discipline and it’s part of what makes them such an efficient team. Alex Chevalier recommended team meetings at the beginning and end of each day - keep everyone updated, motivated, and feeling like one whole team.

Team chants during the full meetings with all the solar car teams are fun. If your chant is easy to discern and to remember, other teams are more likely to join in. I was at the ending banquet where every team was present except Waterloo, and when it was their turn for roll call I did the “water water water” and the entire room echoed back with “loo loo loo” - very powerful moment.

Bring lots of merch to trade! Everyone trades shirts like crazy on the last day. Stickers are also sought after.

### Strategy

The daily schedule of ASC tends to be as follows (they use the 24 hour clock). (The [rules ](https://www.americansolarchallenge.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ASC2026-RegsC.pdf)override my memory if anything is conflicting between the two).

7:00 Unimpound, charging time

9:00 Nominal driving start (could be 8:45 or 9:30 depending on previous day end time)

18:00 Nominal driving end (can end 17:45 to 18:30, will change next day’s start), charging time

20:00 Impound, end charging time

Impound is when the battery is disconnected from solar and locked up with plastic ziptie-like seals. Unimpound is when the seals are cut and solar can be reconnected. Depending on the day there will be checkpoints and stage points to reach. Each “stage” has one or two or three checkpoints and ends with a stage point. If the solar car fails to make it to a checkpoint during the few hours it is “open”, no harm is done (actually I don’t know the rules are confusing); if the solar car fails to make it to a stage point while it’s open, uh oh!! I think they get disqualified, but also the officials might wait or give them extra time if they’re close. After a stage point, cars are released the next morning in minute increments starting at 9:00 in order of miles driven. The solar car gets credit for miles driven on the route during official driving time. If the car breaks down or runs out of battery and has to trailer to reach a stage point, they get moved into the “trailered” category - Washington was the only American team to have avoided trailering for the entire base route.

App State had big 15-person vans for their chase and lead. In their chase, they installed a desk and taped up a monitor in one of the back rows for their strategy team. Other teams like Florida Poly just had someone with a laptop on their lap in the backseat reading out the change in battery every 10 minutes.

Avoid stopping halfway up a hill, because it takes a lot of torque to accelerate against gravity. Solar cars can get stuck for a minute sometimes trying to get going again after a stop. When you dump traffic, pick a spot where there’s a lot of space to keep the solar car moving, and have chase + lead signal with their hands for cars to pass.

### Observer

The observer is a volunteer who notes down your team’s activity and reports to the race officials. They have a clipboard of paper logs, a route book, a GPS, and impound seals. They have to be able to see the car whenever it is unimpounded, that is when charging and when driving and also when being worked on. The observer is also the one to impound and impound the car, so they have to be up at 7 and observe the car until 20:00. They watch the speed of the chase vehicle and use that as an approximation for the solar car’s speed, and thus note down whenever you exceed the speed limit and for how many seconds. They note down all traffic violations such as rolling stops or for how long there are 6 cars behind chase, and any stops for repairs or at a gas station or trailering. Every time you start driving or swap drivers, they have to do a pre-start check: driver name, ballast, helmet, closed-toed shoes, safety belt, horn, signal/lights, driver radio, drinking water, GPS and observer’s luggage in chase, and amber lights working on lead and chase.

There’s a pool of different observers, usually more than the number of teams, that swap out every time you reach an open checkpoint or stage point. Internally, the officials divide the observers loosely into the experienced ones (those who have been observers previously at ASC, ESC, or WSC) and the less experienced ones (often students like myself and Samyak). The experienced ones go to competitive teams like Delft, Innoptus, and ETS so that they don’t take advantage of a squishier observer and try to blow through every red light.

It is specified in the rules and by common decency that you have to be nice to your observer. They have to wake up early, so let them go to sleep early when possible. Often, the more time you spend with them, the more you will like each other - if a team doesn’t make it to any open checkpoints for multiple days in a row, they won’t switch observers and y’all will become best buddies.
